Photosynthesis is the process by which plants, algae, and certain bacteria use light energy to build glucose from carbon dioxide and water. The overall reaction is often summarized as six molecules of carbon dioxide plus six molecules of water producing one molecule of glucose and six molecules of oxygen. In plants, most photosynthesis occurs in the leaves, in a middle tissue layer called the mesophyll. Small openings called stomata allow carbon dioxide to enter and oxygen and water vapor to leave.

Inside mesophyll cells, photosynthesis takes place in organelles called chloroplasts. Each chloroplast is surrounded by a double membrane and contains stacks of flattened sacs known as thylakoids. The fluid surrounding the thylakoids is the stroma. Chloroplasts also contain the green pigment chlorophyll, which absorbs mainly red and blue wavelengths of light and reflects green wavelengths. This reflected light is why most leaves appear green. Accessory pigments, including carotenoids, absorb additional wavelengths.

Photosynthesis has two main stages. The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid membranes. There, pigment molecules in photosystems absorb light, and the energy is used to split water molecules. Splitting water releases electrons, protons, and oxygen. The electrons travel through an electron transport chain, and their energy drives the production of ATP and NADPH. Oxygen leaves the plant through the stomata as a by-product.

The light-independent reactions, also called the Calvin cycle, take place in the stroma. They use ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to convert carbon dioxide into sugars. An enzyme called rubisco attaches carbon dioxide to a five-carbon molecule named RuBP. The resulting unstable compound splits and is rearranged through several steps, eventually producing a three-carbon sugar phosphate. Some of this product is used to regenerate RuBP, and some is used to build glucose and other carbohydrates.

The rate of photosynthesis depends on several limiting factors. Light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration, and temperature can each limit the process when they are in short supply. Water availability also matters, because water is both a reactant and a source of electrons. Some plants have adaptations that reduce water loss. C4 plants separate initial carbon fixation from the Calvin cycle in different cell types, while CAM plants fix carbon dioxide at night and keep their stomata closed during much of the day.

Photosynthesis connects living organisms to the sun and to the atmosphere. It stores energy in organic molecules that consumers can use, and it releases oxygen that supports aerobic respiration. It also removes carbon dioxide from the atmosphere, making it a central part of the global carbon cycle.
