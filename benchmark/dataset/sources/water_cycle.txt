The water cycle, also called the hydrologic cycle, describes how water moves among the ocean, atmosphere, land, and living things. Most of the energy that drives the cycle comes from the sun. Water changes state as it moves, passing between liquid, gas, and solid forms. These changes transfer heat as well as water. When water evaporates, it absorbs energy; when it condenses, it releases energy.

Evaporation is the change from liquid water to water vapor. It occurs from oceans, lakes, rivers, and soil. Over the ocean, evaporation is the largest single source of atmospheric water vapor. On land, a related process called transpiration releases water vapor from plants through small pores in their leaves. Evaporation from soil and transpiration from plants are often combined as evapotranspiration. Sublimation is a smaller pathway in which ice or snow becomes water vapor without first melting.

Rising water vapor cools as it moves to higher altitudes, where lower pressure and temperature cause condensation. Water vapor condenses onto tiny particles called condensation nuclei, such as dust, salt, and smoke, forming clouds and fog. When droplets or ice crystals grow large enough, they fall as precipitation: rain, snow, sleet, or hail. The form of precipitation depends on temperature and on the conditions along the path from cloud to ground.

Some precipitation evaporates before reaching the ground, and some is intercepted by leaves and branches. Of the water that reaches the surface, part flows downhill as runoff toward streams and rivers, and part soaks into the soil through infiltration. Once in the soil, water may be held and later taken up by plant roots, or it may percolate deeper and recharge groundwater. Groundwater moves slowly through pores and cracks in rock and can be stored for long periods in aquifers. Springs and wells draw on groundwater, and streams may gain water where the water table meets the surface.

A watershed, or drainage basin, is an area of land where all surface water drains to a common outlet. Watersheds link precipitation, land use, streams, and groundwater. Impermeable surfaces such as roads and buildings increase runoff and reduce infiltration, which can raise flood risk and lower groundwater recharge. Dams and irrigation alter the timing and location of water movement, while the removal of vegetation can increase erosion and reduce evapotranspiration.

Water spends different amounts of time in each storage pool. Residence time is short in the atmosphere, longer in rivers and shallow soil, and often very long in deep groundwater and glaciers. The ocean contains the largest share of Earth's water and is also the largest source of evaporation. The water cycle is therefore both a global circulation system and a set of local processes that affect agriculture, ecosystems, and water supplies.
