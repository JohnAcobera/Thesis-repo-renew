Plate tectonics is the scientific theory that Earth's outer shell is divided into large rigid plates that move slowly over a weaker layer beneath them. The rigid outer layer is called the lithosphere and includes the crust and the uppermost mantle. Beneath it lies the asthenosphere, a hotter region that deforms slowly and allows the plates to move. The plates fit together like pieces of a shell, and their boundaries are where most earthquakes and volcanoes occur.

There are three main types of plate boundaries. At divergent boundaries, plates move apart and new lithosphere forms as molten rock rises and cools. Mid-ocean ridges are divergent boundaries on the ocean floor, and the process of new crust forming there is called seafloor spreading. At convergent boundaries, plates move toward each other and one plate may descend beneath the other in a process called subduction. Subduction zones produce deep ocean trenches, volcanic mountain chains, and strong earthquakes. Where two continental plates collide, neither subducts easily, and the crust is thickened to form high mountains.

At transform boundaries, plates slide past one another horizontally. The movement is not smooth; friction causes the plates to stick and then slip, releasing energy as earthquakes. The San Andreas Fault in California is a well-known transform boundary. Many transform faults also connect segments of mid-ocean ridges.

The theory grew from earlier ideas, including continental drift. Evidence for moving continents includes matching rock formations and fossils on opposite sides of the Atlantic Ocean, the fit of continental coastlines, and ancient climate patterns found on continents that are now far apart. Later evidence came from paleomagnetism, the study of magnetic patterns recorded in rocks. Magnetic stripes on the seafloor preserve a record of reversals in Earth's magnetic field and support the idea that new crust forms and spreads outward from ridges.

Plate motion is driven by several forces. Mantle convection, the slow circulation of hot rock, is one mechanism. Slab pull, the downward force of a dense subducting plate, is thought to be especially important, while ridge push contributes at spreading centers. These forces are slow but continuous, moving plates at rates of a few centimeters per year, roughly similar to the rate at which fingernails grow.

The consequences of plate tectonics extend beyond earthquakes and volcanoes. The movement of plates shapes continents, opens and closes ocean basins, builds mountain ranges, and influences long-term climate by changing the distribution of land and sea. Understanding plate boundaries is therefore central to assessing earthquake and volcanic hazards and to reconstructing Earth's geological history.
