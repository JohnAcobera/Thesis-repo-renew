Cellular respiration is the set of chemical reactions that cells use to release energy stored in organic molecules and capture that energy in ATP. The overall process is often summarized as glucose plus six molecules of oxygen producing six molecules of carbon dioxide, six molecules of water, and usable energy. Respiration is not a single step but a sequence of pathways, each controlled by specific enzymes. In eukaryotic cells most of these reactions occur in the mitochondria, while the first stage takes place in the cytoplasm.

Glycolysis is the first stage and occurs in the cytoplasm. One molecule of glucose is split into two molecules of pyruvate, and a small amount of ATP and NADH is produced. Glycolysis does not require oxygen, so it can occur in both aerobic and anaerobic conditions. The pyruvate produced in glycolysis is then converted into acetyl coenzyme A before entering the next stage.

The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle, takes place in the mitochondrial matrix. Acetyl coenzyme A combines with a four-carbon molecule and is gradually oxidized. During the cycle, carbon dioxide is released and electrons are transferred to the carrier molecules NADH and FADH2. The cycle also regenerates the starting molecule so that it can accept another acetyl group.

The electron transport chain is located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. Electrons from NADH and FADH2 pass through a series of protein complexes, releasing energy at each step. That energy is used to pump protons across the membrane, creating a difference in proton concentration. The protons then flow back through an enzyme called ATP synthase, which uses the movement to produce ATP. Oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor and combines with electrons and protons to form water. Without oxygen, the chain cannot operate and most ATP production stops.

When oxygen is unavailable, some cells rely on fermentation. In fermentation, pyruvate or its derivatives accept electrons so that glycolysis can continue. Lactic acid fermentation produces lactate, while alcoholic fermentation produces ethanol and carbon dioxide. Fermentation yields far less ATP per glucose molecule than aerobic respiration, but it allows cells to keep producing energy for a short time.

The total ATP yield of aerobic respiration is often estimated at about thirty to thirty-two molecules per glucose molecule, although the exact number varies with the cell type and conditions. Cellular respiration connects the breakdown of food to the energy demands of growth, movement, transport, and maintenance. It also links the carbon cycle to the flow of energy through living organisms.
