An electric circuit is a closed path through which electric charge can flow. A simple circuit contains a source of electrical energy, such as a battery, and one or more components connected by conducting wires. The source creates a difference in electric potential, commonly called voltage, which drives charge through the circuit. Current is the rate at which charge flows, and it is measured in amperes. Resistance opposes the flow of current and is measured in ohms.

Ohm's law describes the relationship among voltage, current, and resistance in many materials. It states that current equals voltage divided by resistance. If the voltage increases while resistance stays the same, the current increases. If resistance increases while voltage stays the same, the current decreases. Ohm's law applies to components that have a constant resistance, but not to every device; some components change resistance as temperature or voltage changes.

Components can be connected in series or in parallel. In a series circuit, there is only one path for the current, so the same current flows through every component. The total resistance is the sum of the individual resistances, and the total voltage is shared among the components. If one component breaks the path, the entire circuit stops. In a parallel circuit, there are multiple paths, and the voltage across each branch is the same. The total current is the sum of the currents in the branches, and the overall resistance is lower than the resistance of any single branch.

Electrical power is the rate at which energy is transferred. It equals voltage multiplied by current, and it can also be calculated from current squared times resistance or from voltage squared divided by resistance. Power is measured in watts. The energy used over time is power multiplied by time, which is why electricity bills are based on kilowatt-hours. A component with high power dissipation converts more electrical energy into other forms, such as light, heat, or motion.

Kirchhoff's laws provide a systematic way to analyze circuits. The junction rule states that the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it, reflecting conservation of charge. The loop rule states that the sum of voltage changes around any closed loop is zero, reflecting conservation of energy. These rules apply to complex circuits even when Ohm's law alone is not enough.

Materials differ in how readily they allow charge to flow. Conductors, such as copper and aluminum, have low resistance, while insulators, such as rubber and glass, have high resistance. Semiconductors have intermediate properties and are the basis of transistors and diodes. Switches control whether a path is open or closed, and fuses or circuit breakers protect against excessive current. Understanding circuits is essential for designing safe and efficient electrical systems.
