The solar system consists of the Sun and the objects that orbit it, including eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets, and dust. The Sun contains almost all of the system's mass and provides the gravitational force that keeps the planets in orbit. It also supplies the light and heat that drive conditions on the planets and their atmospheres. The solar system formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a rotating cloud of gas and dust called the solar nebula.

The four inner planets are Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars. They are called terrestrial planets because they have rocky surfaces and relatively high densities. Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. Venus has a thick atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide and a surface hot enough to melt lead. Earth is the only planet known to support life and has liquid water on its surface. Mars is smaller than Earth and has a thin atmosphere, polar ice caps, and evidence that liquid water once flowed there.

Beyond Mars lies the asteroid belt, a region containing many small rocky bodies. The four outer planets are Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune. They are called gas giants or ice giants because they are much larger and less dense than the terrestrial planets. Jupiter is the largest planet and has a powerful magnetic field and many moons. Saturn is known for its bright ring system, which is made of ice and rock particles. Uranus and Neptune are smaller than Jupiter and Saturn and contain large amounts of water, ammonia, and methane in their interiors.

The planets orbit the Sun in nearly circular ellipses and in roughly the same plane. Their orbital speed decreases with distance from the Sun, as described by Kepler's laws of planetary motion. A planet's year is the time it takes to complete one orbit, while its day is the time it takes to rotate once on its axis. These periods differ widely; Jupiter rotates in less than ten hours, while Venus takes longer to rotate once than to orbit the Sun.

Moons orbit most of the planets. Earth has one moon, while Jupiter and Saturn have dozens each. Some moons are geologically active, and a few may contain subsurface oceans. Dwarf planets, including Pluto, orbit the Sun but have not cleared their orbital neighborhoods of other objects. Comets are icy bodies that develop tails when they approach the Sun, and many originate in the Kuiper belt or the distant Oort cloud.

The solar system is a dynamic system rather than a fixed arrangement. Asteroids and comets can collide with planets, and gravitational interactions gradually change orbits. Understanding the solar system helps explain the history of Earth and the conditions that make a planet habitable.
